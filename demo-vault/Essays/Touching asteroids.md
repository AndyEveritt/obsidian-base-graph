Meteorites land on [[Earth]] already burnt and weathered. To study an asteroid as it really is, you have to go and fetch a piece. [[Sample return]] from the [[Moon]] started with [[Apollo 11]], but asteroids are small, far away and barely hold on to their own surface.

## Hayabusa

[[JAXA]]'s [[Hayabusa]] reached [[Itokawa]] in 2005. Its sampler misfired and it nearly didn't make it home, but in 2010 it landed in Australia with around 1,500 grains of dust from [[Itokawa]]: the first [[Sample return]] from an asteroid.

## Hayabusa2

[[Hayabusa2]] went to [[Ryugu]], a darker, carbon-rich asteroid. In 2019 it fired a copper projectile into [[Ryugu]] to make a crater, then touched down beside it to collect material from below the surface. [[Hayabusa2]] brought 5.4 grams of [[Ryugu]] back in 2020, and [[JAXA]] sent [[Hayabusa2]] on towards two more asteroids.

## OSIRIS-REx

[[OSIRIS-REx]] arrived at [[Bennu]] in 2018 and spent two years mapping it. [[Bennu]] turned out to be covered in boulders, with no clear spot to land. When [[OSIRIS-REx]] finally touched [[Bennu]] in 2020, its sampler sank in as if [[Bennu]]'s surface were a ball pit. It came back with around 120 grams.

[[Bennu]] has a small chance of hitting [[Earth]] in the 2180s, so what [[OSIRIS-REx]] learned about its loose surface matters for [[Planetary defense]] too. [[DART]] tested the other half of that problem in 2022 by moving an asteroid. Between them, [[OSIRIS-REx]] and [[DART]] show how much [[Planetary defense]] depends on knowing what an asteroid is made of.
